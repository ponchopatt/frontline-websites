# Build the free Orthodox Bible app nobody has made

You can build this app, and nobody has filled this gap yet. No free, simple app today puts the Orthodox reading for the day next to what the Church Fathers said about it. Catena Bible has the Fathers but no church calendar. The calendar apps have the readings but no Fathers. Newer apps are moving to subscriptions of **$7.99 to $19.99 a month** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6760005952); [AppsHunter](https://appshunter.io/ios/app/orthodox-christian-bible/id6762108321)). The way to win is to copy YouVersion's simple daily habit (open the app, read today's portion, tick it off) and leave out its clutter, which users openly complain about. The biggest catch is copyright. The old English translations of the Fathers are free to use, but **New Advent's own edited versions carry a "Copyright © 2026 New Advent LLC" notice** ([New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2001.htm)). So use New Advent as your map of what exists and link out to it, but take the actual words from other public-domain copies. The same applies to the Bible: the well-known Orthodox Study Bible is copyrighted, so version 1 should use free texts (Brenton's Septuagint plus the World English Bible). A builder working part-time with AI coding tools can reach a first version ready for the app stores in roughly **4 to 8 weeks**. That figure is the researchers' estimate, not a measured one. The order is: data first, then a simple reader, then the "What the Fathers said" panel, then reading plans.

## Copy YouVersion's daily habit and leave out its clutter

YouVersion is the giant in this space. It is rated **4.9 stars from about 14 million ratings**, it is free, and it offers "thousands of reading plans," audio, offline reading, highlights, notes and verse images ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bible/id282935706)). Its app family passed **1 billion downloads in November 2025** ([ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/news/2209898-youversion-2025-verse-of-the-year-bobby-gruenewald.html)). Two features keep people coming back: a daily home screen that chains Verse of the Day, Guided Scripture and Guided Prayer, and **streaks**, which count days in a row of reading and celebrate milestones like 7 days ([bible.com/refresh](https://www.bible.com/refresh); [Life.Church Open](https://openblog.life.church/3-best-features-in-the-bible-app-for-pastors-to-know-about/)). A YouVersion reading plan is simple underneath: a set number of days, and each day holds a short devotional, one or more Bible passages, and sometimes a video. Partners say plans are usually **5 to 7 days long** ([Agapé UK](https://arc.agape.org.uk/youversion-bible-plans/)).

The complaints matter just as much, because they tell you what to leave out. Reviewers in 2025 said the developers "keep adding functionality and complexity… something new every week," and called the app "cluttered with plans, confusing UI, random people talking that distracts from the Bible" ([Trustpilot](https://au.trustpilot.com/review/bible.com)). Others complain that notes and highlights do not carry over when you switch translations ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bible/id282935706)), and that sync between devices can take days ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/youversion-bible-gateway-olive-tree-logos-blue-letter-bible-apps-ranked-2026)).

So copy these: a single "Today" screen, reading plans as day-by-day checklists, a gentle streak, a daily reminder, highlights, bookmarks and notes, offline reading, verse-image sharing, and the app reopening where you left off. Leave out these: the community feed, church events, giving prompts, friends' activity and the requirement to log in. One idea from YouVersion's own design is worth taking. Every verse has a single label, like `JHN.3.16`, and everything in the app hangs off that label ([bible.com](https://www.bible.com/bible/111/JHN.3.NIV)). Your app can go one better: store each note against the verse itself rather than against one translation, so notes follow you when you switch Bibles. That fixes a known YouVersion complaint. Do not copy YouVersion's plan text or Bible text. Both belong to other people.

## New Advent shows what to include, but take the words from elsewhere

New Advent's Fathers index lists **about 65 named authors**. The authors with the most works are Augustine (48), Chrysostom (36), Tertullian (33) and Athanasius (20). It also has sections for liturgies, the councils and other material ([New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/)). Each work has a number (for example `/fathers/2001.htm` is Chrysostom on Matthew), and each homily inside it adds two digits (`/fathers/200101.htm` through `200190.htm` for all 90 homilies). Those are the 19th-century English translations from the series called the Ante-Nicene Fathers (ANF) and the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (NPNF). The originals are public domain, but every page says "Revised and edited for New Advent by Kevin Knight" and "Copyright © 2026 by New Advent LLC" ([New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2001.htm)). The page that explains New Advent's reuse terms returned an error during the research, so its exact rules are unknown ([attempted URL](https://www.newadvent.org/utility/copyright.htm)). CCEL, the other big host, says its texts may be used for "personal, educational, or non-profit purposes" and asks people to "contact us for permission to republish… or to use them commercially" ([CCEL](https://ccel.org/about/copyright.html)).

The safe approach is to use New Advent as your checklist of what to cover and link out to it as a courtesy. For the text inside your app, use Wikisource transcriptions ([Wikisource ANF](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers/Volume_IV)) or the original scanned books on the Internet Archive ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/cuaantenicenefat01robe/page/28/mode/2up?q=healer)). If you want CCEL's tidy files, get written permission first. One more option is to email Kevin Knight and ask permission outright. It costs nothing and could save months of work.

There is a big shortcut for linking verses to the Fathers. A free project on GitHub called **HistoricalChristianFaith Commentaries-Database** already sorts quotes from the Fathers by Bible verse. It comes as a ready-made database file and is released into the public domain ([GitHub](https://github.com/HistoricalChristianFaith/Commentaries-Database)). It has two traps. Its license says "some commentary excerpts remain under copyright" ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HistoricalChristianFaith/Commentaries-Database/master/LICENSE)), and a sister project uses texts translated with ChatGPT ([Writings-Database](https://github.com/HistoricalChristianFaith/Writings-Database)). Import it, keep only the entries that come from the old ANF/NPNF translations, and drop the rest. Another useful free source is Aquinas's *Catena Aurea*, which strings together the Fathers' comments on each Gospel passage, chapter by chapter ([CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/catena1)).

For an Orthodox audience, it matters which Fathers come first and how each one is labelled. Put **St John Chrysostom first**. His homilies cover Matthew, John, Acts and all of Paul's letters plus Hebrews, which makes him the largest verse-by-verse source and a pillar of Orthodox teaching ([New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/)). After him come the Cappadocians, Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Ephrem and John of Damascus. Augustine needs a careful label, because Orthodox views of him differ. Many link his teaching to the *filioque* (the Western addition to the Creed), yet he is still commemorated on 15 June ([Obitel-Minsk](https://blog.obitel-minsk.com/blog/2020/06/three-mistakes-of-blessed-augustines-theology); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_15_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics))). Mark Tertullian, Origen and Novatian as "early Christian writer, not a saint," and leave the Gnostic texts out of the verse panel completely. Expect one gap. The great later Eastern Fathers (Maximus, Symeon, Gregory Palamas and the full Philokalia) are not on New Advent, and their good English translations are copyrighted ([OrthodoxWiki](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Philokalia); [Faber](https://www.faber.co.uk/product/9780571266180-the-philokalia-vol-4/)). The same is true of Theophylact's Gospel commentary ([OrthodoxWiki](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Theophylact_of_Ohrid)). For these, link out to the books or ask the publishers for permission.

## Brenton's Septuagint and the WEB give a full Orthodox Bible for free

Every English Bible made specifically for Orthodox readers is copyrighted. The **Orthodox Study Bible** uses the NKJV, which Thomas Nelson owns, and it allows only 500 verses without permission ([BibleGateway NKJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/versions/new-king-james-version-NKJV-bible); [HarperCollins permissions](https://www.harpercollinschristian.com/permissions/)). The Eastern Orthodox Bible (EOB) New Testament is © 2013 Laurent Cleenewerck. It is free for parishes and Orthodox members to use non-commercially, but other uses are capped ([EOB PDF](https://www.mauimission.org/static/bibles/EOBBible/EOBBible.pdf)). NETS and the Holy Transfiguration Psalter are also restricted ([NETS](https://septuaginta.uni-goettingen.de/static/ioscs/pdf/nets/copyright.pdf); [Saint Jonah](https://saintjonah.org/services/copyright.htm)).

The free combination is **Brenton's English Septuagint for the Old Testament**, "Published in 1851, and now in the Public Domain" ([eBible](https://ebible.org/find/details.php?id=eng-Brenton)), plus the **World English Bible (WEB) for the New Testament**. The WEB is "not copyrighted," and its edition includes the extra Old Testament books Orthodox read, such as Tobit, Wisdom, Sirach and the Maccabees ([eBible WEB](https://ebible.org/find/details.php?id=eng-web)). The KJV is another free choice outside the UK ([eBible KJV](https://ebible.org/kjv/copr.htm)), and the Greek Patriarchal Text of 1904 is free in Maurice Robinson's files ([GitHub](https://github.com/byztxt/greektext-antoniades)). The trade-off is that Brenton's English is old-fashioned ("thee," "Esaias"). Once the app has users, ask the EOB editor (eobeditor@easternorthodoxbible.org) for permission. His existing parish permission suggests he may be open to a free Orthodox app. Licensing the Orthodox Study Bible would mean a direct deal with HarperCollins, with a 6 to 8 week turnaround just for a reply ([HarperCollins](https://www.harpercollinschristian.com/permissions/)).

One technical point affects everything else: **Orthodox Psalm numbers differ from Protestant ones**. For most Psalms, the Greek number is one lower than the Hebrew/English number, so the Orthodox "Psalm 50" is the English Psalm 51 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalms)). The Fathers also cite Psalms by the Greek number. A free mapping table from STEPBible (CC BY 4.0, which means you must credit them) handles the conversion ([STEPBible-Data](https://github.com/STEPBible/STEPBible-Data)). Show the Orthodox number first, with the English number in brackets, like "Psalm 50 (51)."

## Seven councils, a daily calendar, and plans built on free classics

A reading plan for each council is easy to source. NPNF Series 2, Volume 14 (1900, public domain) contains the creeds, definitions, canons and key letters of **all seven Ecumenical Councils**. It also covers the Council in Trullo (692) and the local councils Orthodox church law accepts, such as Ancyra, Gangra, Laodicea and Carthage ([CCEL NPNF2-14](https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf214/npnf214.toc.html)). New Advent splits the same volume into one page per council. The Nicaea page (3801) has the Creed, all 20 canons and the council's letter ([New Advent 3801](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3801.htm)). OrthodoxWiki gives the short version of each council: Nicaea I (325) against Arius; Constantinople I (381), which completed the Creed; Ephesus (431), which called Mary Theotokos; Chalcedon (451), on Christ's two natures; Constantinople II (553); Constantinople III (680–681), on Christ's two wills; and Nicaea II (787), which restored icons ([OrthodoxWiki](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Ecumenical_Councils)).

A good 7-day plan for each council follows the same pattern: the heresy, then the Bible passages being argued over, then a Father, then the council's decree, then selected canons. For Nicaea, the Father is Athanasius's *On the Incarnation*. Two cautions apply. First, the volume's notes were written by an Anglican editor, so show the primary texts and write your own short Orthodox introductions. Second, the details of each council (emperors, who attended, how many canons) were not checked against an original source during the research, so check them on OrthodoxWiki, OCA.org or GOARCH.org before you publish. Add a bonus plan on the councils of 879–880 and 1341–1351, which some Orthodox call the 8th and 9th ([OCA](https://oca.org/news/headline-news/dr.-david-ford-addresses-otsa-annual-meeting)). Label the Catholic-only councils as "not received by the Orthodox."

The daily Orthodox basics matter even more than the plans. Every Orthodox calendar app shows the day's Epistle and Gospel, the saint of the day, whether it is a fast day, and morning and evening prayers. Each one also asks whether you follow the New or Old Calendar ([orthodoxroad](https://www.orthodoxroad.com/?p=1467); [OrthodoxWiki](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Church_calendar)). You do not have to build that calendar yourself. **Orthocal** is free, MIT-licensed code with a live API that returns the readings, saints, fasting rules and days from Pascha for any date ([GitHub](https://github.com/brianglass/orthocal-python); [orthocal.info](https://orthocal.info/api/gregorian/2026/10/16/)). No terms or usage limits are published, so email the maintainer (Brian Glass) before you rely on it heavily, or run the code yourself. Do not copy saints' lives from OCA.org, which says "All rights reserved" ([OCA](https://www.oca.org/saints/id/lives/108047)). Write a short paragraph for each saint yourself instead. That is a better fit anyway, because users already complain about long biographies.

Other learning plans worth adding, all from free or self-written material: an Intro to Orthodoxy that goes through the Creed with Philaret's Catechism ([OrthodoxWiki](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Online_books)); *On the Incarnation* in 9 days ([CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf205.html)); Great Lent with the Fathers; the Psalter in 20 kathismata (the Orthodox divisions of the Psalter); the Gospels in 90 days with Chrysostom; and short self-written explainers on the sacraments, icons and the church year. Several popular books are copyrighted in English, including the Philokalia, the Prologue of Ohrid and the Jordanville Prayer Book ([Lazarica](https://lazarica.co.uk/?p=1834); [Holy Trinity Publications](https://holytrinitypublications.com/product/prayer-book-9780884651758/)). List them as "further reading." Several older translations may be public domain, including Hapgood's 1906 Service Book, Waddell's *Desert Fathers* and Moore's *Ladder*, but none of them was confirmed in this research. Check each book's title page and the translator's date of death before you use it.

## Build one app for phone and web, and keep version 1 offline

The recommended tool is **Expo (React Native)**. Your AI coding tool can build one codebase that runs on iPhone, Android and the web. The whole Bible and the Fathers' comments go inside the app as one database file, so it works offline and needs no login ([Expo SQLite docs](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/sqlite/)). A free reference-reading library (bcv_parser, MIT license) lets people type "jn 3 16" and jump straight there ([GitHub](https://github.com/openbibleinfo/Bible-Passage-Reference-Parser)). The phone itself can send daily reminders, so you need no server. Skipping accounts in version 1 also keeps you clear of Apple's login and account-deletion rules: Apple says to "let people use it without a login," and any app with accounts must let people delete them in the app ([Apple guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). When you later add syncing across devices, Supabase's free tier allows 50,000 monthly users ([MakerKit](https://makerkit.dev/blog/saas/supabase-pricing)).

The store rules have a few traps. Apple rejects apps that are just "a repackaged website." It bans "inaccurate or misleading quotations of religious texts" and requires permission for copyrighted works ([Apple guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). That last rule turns the copyright plan into a condition for getting into the App Store, not just a legal precaution. Google requires new personal developer accounts to run a **closed test with at least 12 testers for 14 days** before launch ([Google Play Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465?hl=en)). Recruit those testers from a parish early.

Linking verses to Fathers works in three layers, and each layer should be labelled so users know how much to trust it. First, import the cleaned Commentaries-Database (marked "checked"). Second, pull the Bible references printed in the footnotes of the old ANF/NPNF books (marked "cited"). Third, and only later, let AI suggest further links (marked "suggested, not reviewed"). Keep that third layer hidden until a knowledgeable volunteer approves each link. Research shows AI finds these allusions but also produces "false intertextual dependences" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01882)). One study's automated method found only **54%** of the known allusions ([papers.cool](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2604.19447)). Always show the Father's name, the work, the section and a link to the source.

## Top decisions and risks

| # | Decision or risk | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Copying text from New Advent** (copyright notice on every page; reuse terms not found) | Do not copy it in bulk. Link to it, take the text from Wikisource or archive.org scans, and ask Kevin Knight for permission |
| 2 | **CCEL's tidy files** (allowed only for "non-profit purposes"; commercial use needs permission) | Ask in writing. If the app ever shows ads or charges money, assume that is commercial use |
| 3 | **Which Bible translation** (Orthodox Study Bible and EOB are copyrighted) | Launch with Brenton plus the WEB (free). Ask the EOB editor first, and the Orthodox Study Bible's publisher later |
| 4 | **Copyrighted pieces hidden in the Commentaries-Database** | Keep only entries from the old ANF/NPNF translations and remove AI-translated or modern ones |
| 5 | **Orthodox accuracy** (labelling Augustine and Origen; Anglican notes in the council texts; fasting rules differ between jurisdictions) | Label every author. Write your own short introductions. Add a note to "follow your priest's guidance." Have a priest review the plans |
| 6 | **AI features** (AI suggests false links; Orthodox users are sensitive to doctrinal errors) | No AI question-and-answer feature. AI-suggested links stay hidden until a person reviews them |
| 7 | **Calendar data** (Orthocal publishes no terms or limits) | Ask the maintainer, or run the free code yourself |
| 8 | **Saints' lives and prayers** (OCA, Prologue and Jordanville are copyrighted) | Write short lives yourself. Use only prayer translations you have confirmed are public domain |
| 9 | **App store rejection** (copyright, a "website wrapper," the Google 12-tester rule) | Build a real app, keep proof of permissions, and recruit testers from a parish early |
| 10 | **Money model** | Stay free with no ads or tracking. That is your advantage over paid rivals. Apple generally allows in-app donations only from registered nonprofits, so take any donations outside the app |

## Conclusion

The software is the easy part. Free tools and datasets already cover the Bible text, reference reading, Psalm numbering, the Orthodox calendar and a starter set of Fathers' quotes. What will set this app apart is trust: clean permissions, honest labels on every Father and every link, and content an Orthodox priest would be comfortable recommending. That suggests sending a handful of permission emails in week one, before writing any code. A "yes" from New Advent, CCEL or the EOB editor would save weeks of work and remove the biggest legal risk.

The research also points to a design change. YouVersion plans start on whatever day you choose, but the most useful Orthodox plans are tied to the Church year: the daily lectionary, Great Lent, the fasts and the feasts. Build the "Today" screen and the calendar first and treat the other plans as extras. That makes the app something an Orthodox Christian opens every day, rather than just one more Bible reader.
